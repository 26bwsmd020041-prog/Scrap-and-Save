From your counter to the curb

Food waste is a supply chain problem you can actually pull a lever on.

Roughly a third of all food produced worldwide is lost or wasted — and in wealthier households, most of that happens after the food is already home. Here's what actually moves the needle, at three different scales.

01 At home

The cheapest, fastest fixes are the ones already in your kitchen.

  • Shop from your ledger

    Before you order groceries, check what's already logged as "went off early" in your tracker — that's your list of what to buy less of.

  • First in, first eaten

    Move older items to the front of the fridge each time you unpack groceries. A five-second habit that quietly kills the "forgot about it" category.

  • Freeze the in-between

    Ends of bread, herb stems, overripe fruit, and small portions of leftovers all freeze well. Keep one bag for stock scraps, one for smoothie fruit.

  • Cook the fridge, not the recipe

    Once a week, build a meal entirely from what's about to turn. It's the single biggest lever against the "made too much" and "bought too much" categories.

  • Store produce by type

    Ethylene-producing fruit (apples, bananas) ripens everything nearby faster. Keep it separate from leafy greens and herbs to buy them extra days.

  • Portion before you cook

    Cook rice, pasta, and grains by the cup, not by the bag. Oversized batches are the most common reason good food ends up in the bin.

02 In your community

Scraps you can't prevent don't have to be waste — they can become someone else's meal or someone's soil.

  • Start composting

    Kitchen scraps that can't be eaten don't belong in landfill, where they produce methane. A countertop bin plus a local drop-off or backyard pile turns them back into soil.

  • Donate before it turns

    Unopened, unexpired food you won't use goes to food banks and pantries, not the bin. Many accept fresh produce too, not just shelf-stable goods.

  • Join a gleaning group

    Gleaning organizations harvest edible surplus left in fields and orchards after the main harvest and redistribute it locally — a few hours outdoors that directly rescues food.

  • Support "ugly produce" boxes

    Cosmetically imperfect fruit and vegetables are often discarded before they reach a store shelf. Subscribing to a rescue-produce box keeps that food in circulation.

  • Talk to your restaurant

    Ask favorite local restaurants and cafes if they donate unsold food at close, or connect them to an app that redistributes surplus meals nearby.

03 The bigger picture

Individual habits add up, but a lot of waste happens upstream — in grading standards, labeling, and supply chains.

  • Understand date labels

    "Best before" is a quality estimate, not a safety cutoff — unlike "use by." Confusion between the two causes enormous quantities of edible food to be discarded early.

  • Back clearer labeling rules

    Support local and national efforts to standardize date labeling so shoppers and stores stop tossing food out of caution rather than necessity.

  • Push your workplace or school

    Cafeterias and offices generate large, predictable volumes of food waste. Suggest a composting bin, a surplus-sharing shelf, or a donation partnership.

  • Vote with policy in mind

    Food waste reduction targets, organic waste bans, and composting infrastructure are set locally as often as nationally. These are decisions your vote and public comment reach.
